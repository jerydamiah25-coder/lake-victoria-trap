Lake Victoria · Nile perch control

Smart balloon traps that sort the invaders from the natives

Each trap is a clear tube with an AI camera that names every fish swimming through. You decide how many traps sit at each site and what job each one does: a prison that holds Nile perch until a crane boat lifts it out, a sterilizer that stops perch breeding, or a safe refuge where cichlids can shelter while perch are filtered out. Mix them and watch how the whole lake responds.

    Fish factory

    Every Nile perch landed by the fishers or lifted out of a prison trap goes to the factory, where its swim bladder (sold as fish maw) is cut out and sold.

    Reality check: fish maw is usually sold by the kilo, about $38–$200 a kg to local buyers and $450–$1,000 a kg dried abroad, and big bladders fetch the most. In 2018 Kenya, Uganda and Tanzania exported 1,747 t of bladders worth about $124 million. So $200–$400 for every bladder is far above the average. Each perch delivered is counted as a 3 kg market fish.

        Breeding patterns

        January · dry season

        Shaded months are the rainy seasons: long rains March to May, short rains October to December. Most fish breed more when it rains.

        SpeciesHow it breedsWhenHere now

        Only grown females breed, and only eggs fertilised by a fertile male hatch. Young fish are easy prey, except inside a refuge. Brooding mothers show a swollen throat in the lake view, tilapia nests appear on the bottom, and perch eggs drift up as white specks. Growth and clutch sizes are scaled down: a year passes in about 6 minutes at 8×.

            Nile perch vs. cichlids, by day

            Nile perch, left scale Haplochromine cichlids, right scale Rainy seasons

            Nile perch hunt the small cichlids. When perch numbers climb, cichlids fall. Prisons take perch out of the lake; refuges protect cichlids even where perch stay; sterilizers stop perch breeding but leave the adults hunting.

            A teaching model, not a field forecast. Nile perch were introduced to Lake Victoria in the 1950s and helped wipe out hundreds of haplochromine cichlid species. Time is sped up so the effect shows up in a few minutes. The sterilizer and refuge are ideas to explore, not tested devices.

            Where the real-world numbers come from: the lake starts at the 2022 LVFO hydro-acoustic survey (333,341 tonnes of Nile perch, 1,060,589 t of dagaa, cichlids about 15% of a 2.23 million tonne total). Those tonnes are turned into fish using typical weights: surveyed Nile perch are mostly young, averaging about 18 cm (2020 survey), so about 100 g each; cichlids about 10 g; dagaa about 0.8 g. Fishing takes the share of each stock landed in 2021 (LVFO catch assessment: 916,859 t of dagaa, 221,640 t of Nile perch, 58,862 t of cichlids). Dagaa size, maturity and egg counts are from Nyanza Gulf studies (AquaDocs). Today's Nile perch eat mostly shrimp (not shown), young perch and dagaa (Hydrobiologia 2011). Tilapia, ngege and ningu aren't counted by the survey, so their numbers are rough guesses. Nile perch egg counts and size at maturity are from Lake Victoria studies (GRÓ, WorldFish), Nile tilapia from the Nyanza Gulf (FAO AGRIS) and ningu from the Kuja-Migori river basin (NRF Kenya).
